Responsible use of AI

I will be publishing my thoughts on how to responsibly use AI as a junior scholar as I navigate the field.

What Do We Mean by AI as a Method?

November 2, 2026

NoteRead essay

Political scientists increasingly talk about AI as a method, and I am not sure I know what we mean by it. Most of my own use of AI is to assist me in coding. But coding is itself a tool, and when AI helps me with coding, it is not offering a new tool so much as taking over a job that I used to do by hand. That alone does not make AI a method. Using a large language model, especially a reasoning model, to analyze text strikes me as a more legitimate case of AI as a method. Beyond that, I am still trying to work out where the boundaries lie.

This may sound like a purely conceptual question, but I think it is a methodological one that we have to address. How we classify a given use of AI determines what we should ask of it. A tool that drafts our code calls for different scrutiny than a model whose judgments become our data. In what follows, I sort the uses I can think of into a few roles. The list is provisional, and part of my reason for writing it down is to find out what I am missing.

The first role is AI as research labor, i.e., AI as a refinement of the research workflow. Here AI writes and debugs code, cleans and merges data, and handles the routine work of building a code base. This is the role I know best, and in my view it does not change our methods at all. A regression estimated with AI-written code is still a regression. What changes is who did the work and who can answer for it. The issues this role raises are therefore issues of data and workflow management: version control, documentation, and whether a human reader can still follow and contest the code. I took up that last problem in my previous essay, “Code as an Artifact in the Age of AI,” where I argued that AI-assisted code must remain readable to human reviewers.

The second role is AI as a measurement instrument, and this is where I think AI becomes a method in the full sense. When a language model classifies legislative speeches by topic, codes protest events from news reports, or places a party manifesto on an ideological scale, its judgments become our data (Stuhler, Ton, and Ollion 2025; Dahlberg et al. 2026; Lowande and Weiss 2026). Text analysis is the clearest example I have so far, though I doubt it will stay the only one. Once AI is an instrument, it should face the standards we apply to any instrument. We need to know whether it measures what we claim it measures, whether it does so consistently, and what exactly the instrument is. That last point is less trivial than it sounds. A commercial model can be updated or retired, which means the instrument that produced our measures may no longer exist by the time someone tries to replicate them.

The third role is AI as a stand-in for human respondents. Some researchers have asked whether a language model, prompted to take on the characteristics of a demographic group, can answer survey questions the way members of that group would, producing what have been called “silicon samples” (Argyle et al. 2023; Kozlowski and Evans 2025; Timoneda 2026). I am the least sure about this role. It is also the most contested, because it depends on whether a model’s answers represent any real population at all, rather than some average of the text it was trained on. If they do, the implications for survey research are large. If they do not, a silicon sample measures the model, not the public. Can we rely on Generative AI to generate data for our research?

These three roles do not exhaust the possibilities, and at least three more belong on the list. First, AI can serve as a treatment. In some experiments, a chatbot is itself the intervention, e.g., when it tries to persuade respondents or deliberates with them (Bai et al. 2025). The methodological question here is what exactly the treatment is, since no two conversations are alike. Second, AI can act as a generator of ideas, helping researchers form hypotheses or synthesize a literature. This role shapes our theory rather than our data, which makes its influence harder to see and harder to report. Third, AI can act as an auditor, checking whether a replication package runs or reviewing another scholar’s analysis. This role connects back to my essay on code, and it carries the same caution: an AI auditor can help a human reviewer, but if AI both writes and reviews the code, no human may ever have understood it.

Laid side by side, these roles suggest an answer to my opening question. Only some of them make AI a method in the strict sense, namely those in which AI produces or shapes our evidence: as an instrument, as a respondent, or as a treatment. The others are uses of AI as labor, as a source of ideas, or as a reviewer. They do not change our methods, but they do change how we account for our work. This is not to suggest that they matter less. If anything, they are more common and less visible, and they raise accountability questions that our field has only begun to take up.

I expect this list to change, and quickly. That is one reason I am writing these reflections here rather than waiting on a journal’s review cycle. Those of us who are early in our careers and use these tools every day are well placed to notice how they are changing our work, even before we have settled language to describe it. If I have missed a role, I would like to hear about it.

Code as an Artifact in the Age of AI

November 2, 2026

NoteRead essay

One of the first things to be automated in the age of generative AI is coding. As methodologists, and as political scientists more generally, we are still working out what we mean by AI as methodology, but I think one of its most fundamental uses is to help us code more efficiently and more accurately. Yet we usually pay attention to whether our code produces the results we want, and rarely think of the code we used to create those results as an artifact that we have to ship.

Replication is not yet an established fact in our field. Although top journals now require replication materials, most journals in political science do not require a fully replicable code base to be deposited in a repository when we publish. What makes things worse is that, because much of our data is sensitive, some code and raw data cannot be deposited even when we want to share them.

In short, I think we neglect code itself as an important artifact, one that demands our attention in the age of generative AI. So the question I keep coming back to is how much attention we should pay to the code. Based on my own work with co-authors, and with generative AI, I have come to believe that replicable research rests, at its foundation, on transparent coding and transparent data sharing. Even when privacy concerns block data sharing, the coding process should at least be transparent.

We now see more and more researchers report that they use generative AI in coding and that they review their results after the code is generated. I would argue, however, that we need to review not only the accuracy of the code but also its readability. Human reviewers should find it easy to read the code and understand what we are trying to do, even when AI assisted us in writing it.

In my understanding, then, a fully responsible use of AI in coding should produce a fully replicable replication package, one that allows any human reader with an adequate understanding of code to reproduce the results by hand and with their own mind, rather than asking another AI to review what the first AI produced. If we write code that produces accurate results but cannot be read by another human coder, the code becomes, in effect, readable only by another AI. That, I think, is a departure from the responsible use of AI. The concern is heightened by an ongoing debate in the software engineering community over whether AI that writes long stretches of code actually understands it. Some argue that these models only predict the next likely piece of code; others see something closer to genuine reasoning in what they produce. That debate is far from settled, and my argument does not depend on how it resolves: whether or not the AI understands the code, a human reader should be able to.

This essay grew out of a recent conversation with a colleague. I felt that I fully understood, and could fully review, the code that AI agents had produced for me, but to him the same code read as too long and too AI-like to follow. The exchange points to a knowledge gap. The researcher who prompts an AI agent to write code knows what they asked for and why, and so has a much better grasp of the code’s structure. A reader who cannot see the prompt has only the code itself, and may find it far harder to work out what each part does and why it is there. Code can therefore be transparent to its author and opaque to everyone else.

I see two potential ways to close this gap. The first is for human researchers to be able to write and revise the code themselves, so that it stays true to how human coders usually understand code, even when AI helped along the way. The second is to keep the AI-generated code but pair it with detailed documentation of the prompts behind it, or to ask the AI agent to record the instructions it worked from while coding, so that readers have the same context the author had. Personally, I think the first is somewhat easier for researchers to do than the second. Yet as the field keeps evolving, and if the trend is to take coding out of human hands entirely, the second may well become the solution.

I am not saying that we should not use AI to assist with coding. What I am saying is that human authors bear the ultimate responsibility for using AI in the right way, and for making sure that our code is actually reviewable and contestable by human readers. That, I think, matters a great deal.
